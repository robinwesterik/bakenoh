      How Bakenoh V3 picks a winner

      Every horse gets a single score. It starts from the horse's past results, then gains or loses points for each piece of data you enter about it. The highest score is the predicted winner. The method is fixed point tables and hand-written rules, so the same inputs always give the same answer.

      1. Starting mark from past results. Each of the last four races is worth points depending on its class (a G1 counts for far more than a maiden race) and where the horse finished. Winning by a big margin is worth more than a close win; losing by a nose costs less than losing by ten lengths. The record at today's distance is averaged in.
        Probable reason: the newspaper form guide is the most reliable data a 1993 punter had, so it sets the baseline.
      2. A ruler for the race. The game takes the field's average starting mark and divides it by nine. That number is the value of "one point" in this race, so a bonus means the same thing in a race of champions as in a race of maidens.
        Probable reason: keeps all the small bonuses in proportion, whatever class the race is.
      3. Plus and minus points for everything else. Training reports, the jockey, paddock looks, the marks you copied from the paper, sex and age for the distance, best distance and track type, the odds, and a few more. Each is worth roughly −3 to +4 points, multiplied by the weight you set for that item.
        Probable reason: simple tables that a designer could tune by hand and that ran fast on a 4 MHz Game Boy.
      4. Track knowledge. Hand-written rules for each racecourse: closers do well at Tokyo, struggle at Nakayama; inside draws help at Hanshin; a lone front-runner tends to hold on everywhere. Big fields punish the widest draws.
        Probable reason: this is the "expert" content the box sold on, the same wisdom as the course tips you read on screen.
      5. Rank, then pair up. Horses are sorted by total. For bets the game runs a second scoring pass that drops the starting mark and leans on today's signals, then looks at pairs inside the top six of both lists. Pairs are sorted by how much they'd pay (the product of the two odds) into favorite, mid-price and longshot picks.
        Probable reason: in 1993 the popular bets were quinellas (馬連, introduced 1991) and bracket quinellas (枠連); picking pairs by price gives a punter a sensible spread.
      6. A sentence to finish. If many horses score within 20% of the leader the race is "wide open"; if the leader is far ahead it's "a safe bet". The number of non-favorite picks decides whether to promise a big payout.

      When young horses (2-year-olds) or a low-class race are involved, the game switches to a second weight table: form and records are almost ignored and only the jockey, your marks and the odds count, presumably because those horses have too little history to trust.

      The original formula

      Exactly what the cartridge computes for horse h, in whole numbers (every division rounds down).

      score1(h) = B(h) + Σitems ⌊ e(h) · U · w% ⌋ + Σrules r(h) · U
      score2(h) =     0   + Σitems ⌊ e′(h) · U · w% ⌋ + Σrules 2·r(h) · U
       
      B(h) = ½ · ( D(h) + F(h) )
      D(h) = (5000·1st + 2500·2nd + 1000·3rd + 250·unplaced) ÷ (2·runs + 9) · wdistance%  record at today's distance
      F(h) = Σlast 4 races ½ · ( Tfinish[class][place] + Tmargin[class][margin] ) ÷ k · wform%  k = 11, 13, 15, 17 for 1–4 races run
      U = max( 10, ⌊ average B ÷ 9 ⌋ )  the value of one point in this race
      e(h)
      points from the item tables, usually −3 to +4: training, jockey, marks, paddock, sex & age, best distance, best course type, records, popularity, body weight. Pass 2 (e′) uses its own, usually larger, tables.
      records
      exception: course record, racecourse record and heavy-track record add their record score (the same 5000/2500/1000/250 formula as D, divided by 2–10) × w% directly, without U.
      w%
      your item weight, 0–300% (default 100%). In juvenile and ¥9M-and-under races only jockey, marks and odds keep their weight.
      r(h)
      course and race rules, not affected by weights: track bias by running style and draw, lone front-runner, big-field draw penalty, soft-ground outsiders.
      T tables
      a G1 win by 0.3 s or more is worth 6000 + 6000; a maiden win about 100 + 100. Full tables in prediction.md.
      Ranking
      sort by score1 for the finishing order. Bet picks are pairs inside the top 6 of both score1 and score2, ordered by round(oddsA) × round(oddsB).

      How the pieces fit

      Pass 1 also decides the commentary ("Wide open!", "A safe bet!" …) from how close the top scores are.

      Bugs in the 1993 cartridge

      "1993 cartridge" mode reproduces these exactly; "Bugs fixed" mode does what the code clearly meant to do.

      • Body weight is ignored. Two comparisons read a memory address instead of the value stored there, so the game always thinks the weight change is small and inside the horse's winning range. Every horse gets the same body-weight bonus.
      • Paddock counts 250%. The weight lookup wraps around in 8 bits, so the paddock item reads the wrong slot: its default "100%" is really 250%, and high settings drop to 0–30%. Win odds at the top settings drop to 0–30% too.
      • Right-handed dirt is mixed up. The course-record check for right-handed dirt tests for a left-handed race, so at Nakayama or Kyoto dirt nothing counts as "today's course type".
      • Under-rated favorite bonus misfires. The +16 bonus for a marked favorite with a low score uses unsigned maths, so horses with negative scores never get it.
      • Odds-rank ties. The tie check for popularity ranking always compares the same two middle horses.
      • Fresh-horse record. One comparison is the wrong way round, so the best bonus for horses that run well fresh can never be earned.
      • Scratched horses are still counted when ranking by odds and body weight, and "Upset brewing!" can never be shown.
